Storing your records in Australia does not settle which law can reach them. A US statute, 18 U.S.C. 2713, says a provider must disclose “any record or other information pertaining to a customer or subscriber within such provider’s possession, custody, or control, regardless of whether such communication, record, or other information is located within or outside of the United States”.[^1] My read: if a vendor answers to US courts, the location of its data centre is not the end of the question. This is not legal advice.

## The statute turns on control, not location

Who does it apply to? The obligation falls on “A provider of electronic communication service or remote computing service”, and it reaches what is in that provider’s “possession, custody, or control”.[^1] The sentence names location only to set it aside.

Whether a particular point-of-sale, booking or payroll vendor is such a provider is a legal question. I cannot answer it for any vendor.

## Australia and the US have a working agreement

Is there an Australian side? Yes. The two governments signed an agreement on access to electronic data, and a notice on the Federal Register of Legislation records that it “came into force for Australia on 30 January 2024”.[^2]

The Department of Home Affairs describes what it does: “The Australia-US CLOUD Act Agreement enables US agencies to send orders through the US designated authority to Australian communications providers for the purpose of preventing, detecting, investigating or prosecuting serious crime.”[^4] Australian agencies can do the same in the other direction.

In plain terms: since January 2024, orders for data travel directly between the two countries’ agencies and providers, for serious crime.

## What the agreement limits, and what it leaves alone

Does the agreement protect Australian businesses? It limits orders made under it. Article 4.3: “Orders subject to this Agreement shall not intentionally target a Receiving-Party Person”.[^3] Home Affairs says the US is prohibited from targeting Australian persons under the agreement, “including citizens, permanent residents, corporations, non-incorporated associations like charities, government entities and persons physically located in Australia”, and that orders “must relate to criminal offences punishable by at least three years’ imprisonment”.[^4]

The agreement itself compels no one. Article 3.2 says the legal effect of an order “derives solely from the law of the Issuing Party”.[^3] Home Affairs puts it plainly: “The Australia-US CLOUD Act Agreement does not compel providers in either country to comply with an order. However, the domestic law of the issuing country may do so.”[^4]

My read: the targeting limits govern orders made under the agreement. The statute is a separate rule about what a provider must hand over. How the two meet in a given case is a question for a lawyer.

One detail stood out to me. The Home Affairs page, last updated 8 October 2026, still says entry into force is “anticipated to be by the end of 2022”.[^4] The notice is the document to rely on.

## What one vendor’s notice says

Square’s seller privacy notice is a typical example. It says Square may disclose information to service providers outside Australia, “including, but not limited to, in the United States, Canada, Japan, Ireland, France, Spain and the UK”. It also discloses information where “reasonably necessary (i) to comply with any applicable law, regulation, legal process or governmental request”.[^5] It does not say which country’s law counts as applicable.

## For your AI lead

My read, in order:

1. List every vendor that holds guest, staff or sales records: the company you contract with, its parent, and where each is incorporated.

2. For each, record where data is stored and where it is processed, including backups, support access and any AI features.

3. Record each vendor’s clause on government requests, and whether it promises to tell you.

4. Treat Australian hosting as one answer, not the whole one.

## What this does not show

- Whether any particular vendor is a provider under 18 U.S.C. 2713. That is a legal question, and this is not legal advice.

- How often orders are made, or whether any vendor serving your venues has received one.

- How the agreement’s targeting limits and the statute interact in a given case.

- Square’s corporate home. The notice does not state it, so Square appears here only as an example of a transfer clause.

## Put this in writing

Send this to each vendor that holds guest or staff data, and file the replies with the contracts.

> Subject: Government requests for our data
>
> For each product you supply to us, please confirm in writing:
>
> 1. Which company holds our data, and where are it and its parent company incorporated?
> 2. Where is our data stored and processed, including backups and support access?
> 3. If a government outside Australia orders you to disclose our data, which law would you follow, and will you tell us first unless the law forbids it?
> 4. Do you publish a report of the government requests you receive, and where?
