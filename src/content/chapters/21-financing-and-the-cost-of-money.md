> I am not a financial adviser or a lawyer. This chapter states what has changed in the market. Decisions belong with your accountant and lender.

On 3 November 2026 the Reserve Bank of Australia’s Monetary Policy Board increased the cash rate target by 25 basis points to 4.85 per cent. Six members voted for the increase and three voted to leave the rate at 4.60 per cent.[^1] The Board said inflation “is not expected to reach the top of the 2–3 per cent target range until late 2027”, around three quarters later than it had forecast.[^1]

A cash rate is the rate the central bank sets for overnight lending between banks. It is the starting point for what banks charge on business loans. This chapter reads what the sector’s own failure data shows, what the RBA changed in card payments, and what one POS-linked loan costs.

## The sector was already stretched

CreditorWatch, which sells business credit data, reports that in the 12 months to July 2026 the closure rate for cafés, restaurants and takeaway businesses was 12.03 per cent, against 6.69 per cent across all industries.[^2]

Its forward indicators are worse than the national ones. In July 2026, 10.21 per cent of those businesses were “60 or more days in arrears on their payments”, against 5.36 per cent nationally. The trade payment default rate was 1.15 per cent, “close to four times the national average of 0.31%”.[^2]

CreditorWatch’s own reading is that arrears and defaults signal closures before they happen. That is a vendor’s interpretation of its own data, and I have not tested it.

The Fair Work Commission’s wage decision of 2 June 2026 points the same way. In the sectors where award-reliant employees are most concentrated, which include Accommodation and food services, it says business conditions improved and, “except in restaurants, cafes and catering, profit margins were healthy”. It adds that the uncertainty from the Middle East conflict, “as well as the effect of increasing interest rates, is likely to put pressure on business”.[^3]

## What the surcharge ban changes for costs

From 1 October 2026 the RBA has removed surcharging on eftpos, Mastercard and Visa, and lowered the interchange caps on domestic card transactions. It estimates the changes lower merchants’ wholesale card payment costs by around $910 million a year, counting a cap on foreign cards that starts on 1 April 2027.[^4] The RBA estimates that around 16 per cent of merchants surcharged on these cards in 2024/25.[^4] Chapter 8 covers what that means at the terminal.

## Funding that is repaid out of card sales

Square’s Australian loans show how a POS-linked loan works. Square says its loans are “designed to be paid back within 12 months”, with a fixed percentage of daily card sales applied to the balance. It also says: “The cost of your loan does not change by making prepayments.”[^5]

My read: ask for the loan’s effective annual cost, not only its fee. A fixed fee repaid over six months costs more per year than the same fee over twelve. I did not find the fee in dollars on Square’s pages.

## What I take from it (my read)

- Work out what a one-point rise in your loan rate does to the monthly repayment on every loan and lease you hold. Do it before the next rate meeting, not after.

- Ask what happens to the loan if you change POS or payments provider.

- Keep a cash buffer sized to your fixed costs. I have no source for a particular number of months.

- Raise funding while you can show clean numbers. Lenders look at lease terms, cash flow and guarantees. I have not read any lender’s criteria for venues.

## Questions to put in writing

Send these to a lender or finance provider before you sign.

1. Is the rate fixed or variable, and when can it change?

2. What is the total I will repay in dollars, and the effective annual rate?

3. Is a personal guarantee required, and over which assets?

4. What happens if a repayment is missed, and what fees apply?

5. What happens to this facility if I change my POS, payments or accounting provider?
