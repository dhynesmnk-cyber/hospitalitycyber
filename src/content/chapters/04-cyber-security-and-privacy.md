In late October 2026, RMS Cloud, which supplies property management and booking systems to “more than 6,000 hotels, holiday parks, and serviced apartments globally”, said it was investigating phishing messages that “reference genuine booking information”. Scammers had used real guest booking data to send them.[^1] Two months earlier Quest Apartment Hotels reported a breach that came through a third-party service provider.[^2] In both, the attack landed on a vendor and the guests contacted the venue.

This chapter reads what the record shows, what a draft law would change, and one survey of how hospitality is protected.

## Two breaches, both through a vendor

RMS Cloud said: “No payment card information has been exposed from RMS.” A BIG4 spokeswoman said the breach came from “a third-party platform which is used by some of the parks within our network”, and that some guests received fraudulent messages “asking for credit card or payment details”.[^1] The reports I read do not give a count of affected guests or say how the attackers got in.

Quest said on 19 August 2026 that it had “identified unauthorised access to a database system arising from a vulnerability through a third-party service provider”. Its letter of 16 September counted about 1,991,613 customers, including 46,727 credit card numbers with CVV.[^2][^3] Its first statement had said the information “primarily involves names, email addresses, and/or other contact details”.

## The numbers

Australia recorded 1,205 notifications under the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme in 2025, the highest since the scheme began in 2018 and 8 per cent more than in 2024, according to IDM’s report of the OAIC’s statistics. Of those, 716 were malicious or criminal.[^4] I could not open the OAIC’s own release.

The Council of Small Business Organisations Australia (COSBOA) surveyed more than 1,570 small-business owners and employees in 2026. Among hospitality respondents, 47 per cent reported using unique passwords or phrases and 37 per cent said they have cloud-based backups. About one-third have multifactor authentication on email.[^5] That is a survey of respondents, reported by an insurance trade publication.

## What the law says, and what is proposed

The Privacy Amendment (Personal Data Protection) Bill 2026 is an exposure draft, which means it is a proposal released for comment, not law. Hall & Wilcox report that it would require eligible data breaches to be reported to the OAIC “within 72 hours of becoming aware of reasonable grounds to believe that an eligible data breach has occurred”. The existing 30-day assessment period would stay.[^6]

From 10 December 2026, an entity covered by the Australian Privacy Principles must describe certain decisions made by a computer program in its privacy policy.[^7] Chapter 6 covers the rules for smart devices.

## What I take from it (my read)

The basis is the survey gaps above and the vendor breaches.

- Use a unique password for every login, in a password manager, with multifactor authentication on email, the POS back office, payroll and the bank.

- Keep backups of anything you cannot lose, and test restoring one.

- Write a one-page incident plan: who calls whom, in what order, including your vendors.

- List every vendor that holds guest or staff data. A breach there reaches you.

- Practise phishing with staff, including casuals.

## Questions to put in writing

Send these to each vendor that holds guest or staff data.

1. Within how many hours of confirming an incident that touches our data will you tell us, and how?

2. Will you update us with counts and categories as you confirm them?

3. Which categories of our data do you hold, and what is the deletion schedule for each?

4. Which third-party providers hold or process it, and will you tell us when an incident involves one?
