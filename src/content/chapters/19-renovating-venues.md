A renovation is cheaper than a new build and riskier than it looks, because you trade while it happens or you do not trade at all.

This is a background chapter. It draws on industry practice, so most of it is my read. The one sourced figure is the cost of money: on 3 November 2026 the Reserve Bank raised the cash rate target by 25 basis points to 4.85 per cent.[^1] Chapter 21 covers funding.

## What to check before committing (my read)

- **The lease.** Who pays, who approves, and what must be reinstated when you leave.

- **The code.** Work that touches structure, fire, kitchen exhaust or accessibility can trigger upgrades to the current code. Get a certifier’s view before the design is final. Chapter 18 notes that the applicable code edition differs by state.

- **Trading through the works.** Staged works, temporary signage, and any rent relief from the landlord.

- **Contingency.** Older buildings hide defects in plumbing, wiring and materials. Set aside a contingency, and I have no sourced percentage to give.

## The cheapest time for technology

While walls are open is the cheapest time to replace POS terminals, add Wi-Fi coverage, run cabling and fit sensors. Schedule that work with the building work, not after it.

## Questions to put in writing

Send these to your builder, landlord and lender.

1. What is covered by the fixed price, and what is a variation?

2. What happens to the programme and the price if a hidden defect is found?

3. What does the lease require at the end, and does the landlord approve the design in writing?

4. Will the lender fund works while we continue to trade, and on what terms?

## What this chapter does not show

- Renovation costs, contingency levels or lead times. I found no source I could read.

- The code, the licences or the approvals that apply in your state.

- Energy incentives or what a reopening does for press coverage.
