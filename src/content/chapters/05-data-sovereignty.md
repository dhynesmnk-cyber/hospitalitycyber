A US law added on 23 March 2018 reaches records held by a provider “within such provider’s possession, custody, or control, regardless of whether such communication, record, or other information is located within or outside of the United States”.[^1] So a server in Sydney does not settle whose law applies to the data on it.

This chapter separates where data sits from who can be made to produce it, and shows what a few vendors say about both. I am not a lawyer, and I have not tested how that statute applies to any vendor named here.

## Residency is not sovereignty

Residency is where data is stored. Sovereignty is who controls it and whose law governs it. Vendors have started selling “sovereign” offerings, and critics have a name for the ones they call cosmetic. Lawfare (9 October 2025) describes “sovereignty washing” as “offering a service that cosmetically addresses data governance without any meaningful sovereign control for European regulators”.[^2] That article is about European regulators. My read: the same test applies to any vendor’s “Australian” label. Ask what it changes, and what it leaves alone.

## Where two vendors say your data goes

Square’s seller privacy notice, last updated 17 September 2026, says it may “disclose your information to third-party service providers outside of Australia for the purpose of processing and storing your information, including, but not limited to, in the United States, Canada, Japan, Ireland, France, Spain and the UK”.[^3]

Shopify says in its privacy policy, updated 7 July 2026, that it is a Canadian company and may send personal data to other countries, including the United States.[^4]

Both are sentences about disclosure, and neither says who can read the data once it is there. I read these two notices. I have not surveyed others.

## What vendors and government are offering

On 4 November 2025 Microsoft said it would offer customers in four countries, Australia among them, the option of having Microsoft 365 Copilot interactions processed in-country by the end of 2025. An editor’s note of 3 April 2026 on the same page says local processing “is expected to become available in Australia” by the end of 2026.[^5]

The federal government’s cloud policy applies to agencies from July 2026, according to iTnews. It applies to government, not to a venue.[^6]

## What the Privacy Act now says

The Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 lets the government prescribe countries whose privacy laws are substantially similar to the Australian Privacy Principles. From 10 December 2026, the privacy policy of an entity covered by the Australian Privacy Principles must describe certain automated decisions that could significantly affect a person.[^7] I did not check whether any country has been prescribed.

## What changes when you add AI

Square’s Generative AI Terms say that any Input you provide “may be shared with such third party service providers”.[^8] The terms name no providers and no countries. My read: an AI tool fed with guest records adds another provider, and another place the data can go, to the list you need to ask about. Before paying extra for a “sovereign” label, work out which of the questions below it answers.

## Questions to put in writing

Send these to every vendor that holds guest or staff data. For a small venue my read is that this list does more than a change of cloud.

1. Where is our data stored, and where is it backed up?
2. Who can access it, and from which countries?
3. Who holds the encryption keys?
4. Which foreign laws can compel you to disclose it?
5. Which third-party providers process it, and will you tell us if that changes?
6. What happens to our data when we leave, and how fast?

## What this chapter does not show

- Where any particular vendor holds your data. I read two notices, from Square and Shopify.

- Whether any country has been prescribed under the Privacy Act.

- Whether Microsoft’s Australian date has moved since 16 November 2026.

- The text of the federal cloud policy. iTnews is a secondary source, and I could not open the DTA’s own page.
