Arts Law, an Australian legal service for creators, says that “a work can only be protected by copyright in Australia if there is a human author who contributed ‘independent intellectual effort’”.[^1]

For a venue, that decides whether a logo or a menu photograph made with an AI tool is something you own and can stop others copying. This chapter is short because most of what circulates about design tools is vendor pricing and blog opinion that I could not source. What follows is the part that matters for ownership.

## What Arts Law says

“Since this technology is so new, it is not clear that works created with the help of AI will be protected by copyright.” If a work generated by an AI tool has too little human input, Arts Law says, it may not be protected. If a creator produced an image and used an AI tool to edit it, “the human contribution is likely to be enough”.[^1]

Arts Law adds that “AI tools do not currently have a legal status and cannot own copyright. It is the human contributor who would own copyright if a work was protected.”[^1] It says it is unclear whether other contributions, such as writing prompts, tweaking outputs and training the tool, are enough.

My read: a logo that is the unedited output of a generator is a weak asset, because you may not be able to stop a competitor using something near-identical. That is an inference from Arts Law’s general rule, not a legal opinion, and I am not a lawyer.

## What I take from it (my read)

- Commission the logo and core identity from a designer, and keep the source files and a written licence or assignment.

- Use templates for weekly posts, with the logo, colours and fonts locked.

- Keep one brand folder: logo files, colours, fonts, photography and tone of voice.

- Photograph the real menu, and show the real dish. I have not read the consumer law on misleading images, so I make no claim about it.

- Check alcohol and allergen content before anything is printed. I did not research the rules.

## Questions to put in writing

Send these to a designer or an AI design vendor.

1. Who owns the copyright in the finished work, and will you assign it to us in writing?

2. Did you use AI tools, and for which parts of the work?

3. Do you supply the source files and the fonts’ and images’ licences?

4. For an AI design product: what do your terms say about commercial use of what it produces, and about what it does with what we upload?

## What this chapter does not show

- What any design tool costs. The prices I had were single-source and unverified.

- Trade mark law for logos, which chapter 20 touches on.

- The consumer law on food images, alcohol advertising codes, or labelling standards.

- Whether the Copyright Act will change. Arts Law’s sheet describes the position as unclear.
