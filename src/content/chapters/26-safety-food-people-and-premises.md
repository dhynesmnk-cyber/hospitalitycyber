On 1 December 2025 the Occupational Health and Safety (Psychological Health) Regulations 2025 began for all Victorian employers.[^1][^2] On 1 July 2026 section 26A of the Work Health and Safety Act 2011 began in New South Wales, making codes of practice the minimum standard a business is expected to meet.[^3]

Both reach hospitality, where guest aggression, long shifts and fatigue are everyday hazards. A psychosocial hazard is a work condition that can harm mental health, such as bullying, aggression or long hours. This chapter reads what each rule says, and what FSANZ requires for food.

## Victoria: psychological health

WorkSafe Victoria says the regulations “create new obligations for workplaces across Victoria to protect employees from psychological injury” and that a compliance code was published with practical guidance.[^1] The Minister’s release lists psychosocial hazards as including “bullying, sexual harassment, aggression or violence, and exposure to traumatic events or content”.[^2]

## New South Wales: codes of practice

SafeWork NSW says that with section 26A in force from 1 July 2026, “Codes become the minimum performance standards that a PCBU is expected to comply with”. A PCBU is the business or undertaking that owes the duty. The duty is to comply with a code or manage the risk in a way that is equivalent or higher.[^3] I did not read the psychosocial code itself, or the position in other states.

## Food: temperature

FSANZ: “You must ensure that the temperature of potentially hazardous food is either at 5°C or colder or at 60°C or hotter when it is received, displayed, transported or stored.” Food to be cooled must go “from 60°C to 21°C in a maximum of two hours and from 21°C to 5°C within a further maximum period of four hours”.[^4] Chapter 6 covers sensors that log it.

## What I take from it (my read)

- Treat guest aggression, rosters and fatigue as hazards to assess and write down, the way you do a hot kitchen.

- Train managers to handle an incident and to record it, and keep the record. A register that shows no action is worse than none.

- Automate cold chain alerts and test them. A temperature log is only useful if somebody sees the alarm.

- Add an IT outage to the emergency plan. If the POS, locks or cold chain monitoring go down, it is a safety event as well as a trading one.

## Questions to put in writing

Send these to your insurer and to any safety or incident-reporting vendor.

1. Which of the Victorian and NSW rules does your cover or your product assume we meet?

2. Where is incident and staff data held, who can see it, and how long is it kept?

3. What does the product alert us to, who receives the alert, and what happens if nobody responds?
